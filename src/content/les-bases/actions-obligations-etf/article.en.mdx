Three instruments keep coming up whenever investing is discussed: stocks, bonds, ETFs. They are three very different ways to put money to work, with risk and return profiles that have nothing in common.

## Stocks: a residual ownership stake

A stock is a share of a company's capital. Being a shareholder means being a co-owner, with two concrete consequences:

- You're entitled to a share of profits, paid out as dividends if the company decides to distribute any (it's never obligated to).
- In case of bankruptcy, shareholders are paid last, after creditors and bondholders. That's what "residual" means: you get what's left, potentially nothing.

This explains both a stock's high upside potential (the company can multiply its value) and its symmetric downside (it can also lose all of it).

## Bonds: a loan with terms fixed in advance

Buying a bond means lending money to an issuer (a company or a government) that commits to paying you fixed interest (the coupon) and repaying the principal on a set date (maturity). Unlike a stock, you're not a co-owner: you're a creditor, ranking above shareholders if things go wrong.

The price of an already-issued bond moves on the secondary market based on interest rates: if rates rise, an existing bond paying a lower fixed coupon becomes less attractive, so its price falls to compensate. This is a mechanical effect, not a judgment on the issuer's quality.

<Callout>
A misconception worth correcting: a bond is not "risk-free". Default risk exists (the issuer fails to repay), and interest rate risk exists too (resale value drops if rates rise). What is true is that a solid government bond has a markedly lower risk profile than a stock.
</Callout>

## ETFs: a basket instead of a single bet

An ETF (Exchange-Traded Fund) tracks the performance of an index, for example the MSCI World (roughly 1,500 companies across developed countries) or the CAC 40. Instead of buying a single stock and betting on a single company, you buy a fraction of hundreds or thousands of companies at once, in one transaction.

Two replication mechanisms exist, with an important nuance we'll go deeper on in the tax wrappers module:

- **Physical replication**: the fund actually holds the index's stocks.
- **Synthetic replication**: the fund holds a different basket of securities and enters a swap contract with a bank to receive the exact performance of the target index. This is the replication type that makes an S&P 500 ETF possible inside a PEA, even though US stocks aren't directly eligible in one.

<Example title="Quick comparison">
A single Apple stock: total concentration on one company, high upside potential, possible total loss.

An MSCI World ETF: exposure to roughly 1,500 companies across 23 developed countries in a single line, the total loss risk of any single company becomes irrelevant (a bankruptcy is only a tiny fraction of the fund), returns that track the global market average.

A 10-year French government bond: known fixed income ahead of time, very low default risk, but capped return and sensitive to rate movements.
</Example>

<KeyTakeaway label="Key takeaway">
Stocks offer high upside potential with the risk of total loss on a single company. Bonds offer predictable income with lower but capped return. ETFs turn a bet on one company into exposure to an entire market, which is why they serve as the building block for most self-directed investors' strategies.
</KeyTakeaway>
