"Risk" gets treated as a single block in most investing discussions, as if everything that makes a portfolio drop were equivalent. That's wrong, and it's probably the confusion that costs individual investors the most. There are at least three different risks, and mixing them up leads to the worst decisions.

## Risk 1: market risk (volatility)

An asset's price moves, sometimes a lot, both up and down. That's volatility. A World ETF can lose 30 to 40% of its value during a crash, then recover its initial level and go beyond it in the following years. Historically, that's what has always happened after every major correction in the global stock market.

This risk is **temporary in principle**, under one strict condition: not being forced to sell at the wrong time. If you don't need to liquidate your position during the downturn, a market drop isn't a loss, it's a fluctuation. It only becomes a real loss if you sell while it's down.

<Callout tone="warning">
This is where most of the damage happens: not because of the drop itself, but because of panic-selling during the drop, which turns a temporary fluctuation into a permanent loss.
</Callout>

## Risk 2: permanent loss

A company goes bankrupt and its stock becomes worthless. A bond issuer defaults. A financial counterparty fails to honor a commitment. Unlike market risk, this one doesn't "heal" with time: the money lost is gone.

This is the risk to minimize structurally, and the main tool for that is diversification: holding an ETF of 1,500 companies instead of a single stock makes any one company's bankruptcy negligible to the overall portfolio.

## Risk 3: the risk you didn't look for

The most dangerous of the three, because it's invisible until you've actively looked for it. Two concrete examples, covered in more depth elsewhere on this site:

- A synthetic-replication ETF carries counterparty risk on its swap contract, on top of ordinary market risk (see the PEA module).
- An illiquid fund can force you to sell at a degraded price if you need to exit quickly (see the article on financial markets).

<Example title="Why time horizon changes everything">
An investor compounding over 20 years with no specific withdrawal date can absorb a 40% drop without it being a real problem: they never need to sell at the bottom. The same investor, if they need to withdraw their capital in 8 months for a home purchase, cannot afford the same exposure: ordinary market risk becomes, for them, a real risk of not having the expected amount at the right time.

Risk is therefore never a fixed property of an asset. It also depends on the situation of the person holding it.
</Example>

<KeyTakeaway label="Key takeaway">
Always distinguish: market risk (temporary, acceptable if your horizon allows it), permanent loss risk (to minimize through diversification), and unidentified risk (to actively hunt down before investing). The right question before any investment isn't "is this risky", it's "what exact risk is this, and am I in a position to accept it".
</KeyTakeaway>
