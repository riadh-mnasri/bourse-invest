A financial market does one thing: it connects someone with spare money today to someone who needs money today, in exchange for a promise of value over time. A company needs capital to build a factory or hire people, a government needs capital to fund its budget, a saver has a surplus they don't need to spend right away. The market brings the two together.

## Primary market vs secondary market

This is the single most useful distinction to hold in mind, because it changes what "buying a stock" actually means.

The **primary market** is the moment a security is issued for the first time: a company going public (IPO) or raising new capital, a government issuing a new bond. It's the only moment where the money you pay actually goes to the issuer.

The **secondary market** is everything that happens afterward. When you buy a Total or LVMH share in your brokerage account on a Tuesday afternoon, you're not buying anything from the company: you're buying the security from another investor who decided to sell, at a price you both agreed on. The company doesn't see a single cent of that transaction. What you're buying is an already-existing ownership stake, not fresh financing.

<Callout>
This distinction often surprises people: day-to-day "investing in the stock market" for an individual is almost never directly financing a company. It's exchanging ownership stakes between investors on the secondary market. Company financing happens upstream, at the point of issuance.
</Callout>

## How a price forms

A stock market works like a continuous auction. At every moment, there's an order book: buyers proposing a price (the "bid") and sellers asking for another (the "ask"). A trade happens when the two meet. The displayed price isn't a value set by some authority, it's the last point where supply and demand actually crossed.

<Example title="A simple example">
You want to buy a stock at €50. If nobody is willing to sell at that price, your order sits in the book until a seller accepts it, or until you accept the price an existing seller is asking. The displayed price keeps moving, trade after trade, reflecting what participants are willing to pay or accept at that exact moment.
</Example>

## Liquidity: the invisible parameter

Liquidity measures how easily you can buy or sell an asset quickly without moving its price significantly. A CAC 40 stock or a large World ETF are very liquid: thousands of trades a day, a tiny bid/ask spread. A small, thinly-followed stock, or an obscure fund, can be much less liquid: few buyers on the other side, a wider price spread, a risk of having to sell for less than expected if you need to exit quickly.

This isn't a technical detail reserved for professionals: it directly connects to one of the risks we'll go deeper on later, the risk of not being able to exit a position at the price you expected.

<KeyTakeaway label="Key takeaway">
Buying a stock is almost always buying an already-existing ownership stake from another investor, not directly financing a company. The price is the continuous result of buyers and sellers meeting, not a value handed down from above. And how easily you can exit a position (liquidity) matters just as much as the price you got in at.
</KeyTakeaway>
