Robert Kiyosaki builds the whole of *Rich Dad Poor Dad* around a single distinction, repeated in different forms throughout the book: the difference between an asset and a liability isn't read on a price tag, it's read in the direction cash flows.

## The definition that changes how you read everything

An **asset** puts money in your pocket: an ETF that pays dividends, a rental property whose rent exceeds its costs, a share of a company that distributes profits. A **liability** takes money out: a consumer loan, a car that loses value and costs money to maintain, a primary residence on which you pay charges and property taxes without it generating any income.

The book's deliberately provocative point: a house you live in, under this strict definition, is a liability, not an asset, as long as it generates no income. This isn't a judgment on buying real estate as such, it's a reminder that the price paid or the displayed value says nothing about the direction of the cash flow it generates day to day.

<Callout>
This distinction isn't an official accounting rule, it's a reading tool. Its value is forcing the question "will what I'm buying earn me money, or cost me money", before looking at the sticker price.
</Callout>

## Why this reframes an ETF portfolio

A World ETF held over time is an asset in Kiyosaki's sense: it generates compounded return over time, without consuming monthly cash once bought. That's the structural difference between "accumulating assets that work for you" and "accumulating expenses that look like an investment" (a sports car financed on credit isn't an asset, even if it projects an image of success).

<Example title="The test to apply">
Before a major purchase, ask the question in the book's terms: "over time, will this expense put money in my pocket (asset) or take it out (liability)?" A monthly contribution to a World ETF clearly falls in the first category. A revolving credit line for a consumer good clearly falls in the second.
</Example>

<KeyTakeaway label="Key takeaway">
The question to ask isn't "how much does it cost" but "which direction does the money flow, once the purchase is made". It's the simplest filter to sort, within a budget, what builds wealth from what doesn't.
</KeyTakeaway>
