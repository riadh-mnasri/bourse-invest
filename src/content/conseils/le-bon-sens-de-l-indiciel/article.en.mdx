John Bogle founded Vanguard in 1975 and created the first index fund available to individual investors. In *The Little Book of Common Sense Investing*, he makes a simple case, backed by decades of data: trying to beat the market costs more than it pays off, for the vast majority of investors, professionals included.

## The core argument: fees compound too

An active manager charging 1.5% in annual fees first needs to generate 1.5% of outperformance just to match an index fund charging 0.2%. Over a single year, the gap looks small. Compounded over 20 or 30 years, it becomes considerable, because fees get taken out every single year from capital that should have kept compounding.

<Example title="The effect of fees over time">
€100,000 invested for 30 years at an identical gross return of 7% a year: with 0.2% annual fees (6.8% net return), the final capital is around €730,000. With 1.5% annual fees (5.5% net return), it's around €500,000, for the exact same gross market performance. The roughly €230,000 gap comes from no difference in market performance whatsoever, purely from fees taken each year.
</Example>

## Why beating the market is so rare

Bogle documents, with decades of data, that a large majority of actively managed funds underperform their benchmark index over the long run, once fees are deducted. It's not that managers are incompetent: it's that collectively, active investors *are* the market, so their average performance before fees is necessarily the market's, and becomes worse once fees are subtracted.

<Callout>
Bogle's most-quoted line: don't look for the needle in the haystack, just buy the haystack. A broad index ETF does exactly that: instead of guessing which companies will outperform, you hold the market as a whole.
</Callout>

## What this means in practice

This isn't an argument against all active management or stock-picking (see the core-satellite allocation module, which deliberately leaves room for the satellite), it's an argument for the core of a portfolio resting on low-fee index funds rather than an attempt to pick the best companies or the best managers.

<KeyTakeaway label="Key takeaway">
The most reliable and most controllable driver of long-term performance isn't selection talent, it's the fee level paid year after year. A low-fee World ETF (generally under 0.25% a year) remains, decades after Bogle's thesis, the most robust tool for a portfolio's core.
</KeyTakeaway>
