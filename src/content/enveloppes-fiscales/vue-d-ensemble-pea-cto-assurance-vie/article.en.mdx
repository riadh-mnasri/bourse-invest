A tax wrapper is not an investment product: it's a legal and tax framework in which you hold investments. The same stock or ETF can sit inside a PEA, an ordinary brokerage account (CTO), or a life insurance contract (assurance-vie), with completely different tax treatment depending on which wrapper you chose.

## The PEA: the most favorable tax treatment, with real constraints

The PEA (Plan d'Épargne en Actions) lets you invest in **EU-eligible** stocks and ETFs (see the dedicated article for what that actually means in practice) with a capital gains tax exemption after 5 years of holding (social contributions still apply). In exchange: a €150,000 contribution cap, and a withdrawal before 5 years closes the plan and loses the tax benefit in most cases.

## The CTO: no restrictions, less favorable tax treatment

The ordinary brokerage account accepts any listed security in the world, with no cap and no holding-period constraint. In exchange, every realized gain is immediately subject to the flat tax of 30% (12.8% income tax + 17.2% social contributions), with no deferral possible. This is the wrapper to use for anything the PEA doesn't allow: US or Asian stocks held directly, certain ETFs not eligible for the PEA.

## Life insurance: the most versatile, the most complex

A French life insurance contract holds both "fonds en euros" (capital-guaranteed, low return) and "unités de compte" (stocks, ETFs, real estate, not guaranteed). Its tax treatment improves with the contract's age, with an annual allowance on gains after 8 years, and it offers a favorable framework for wealth transfer (outside standard inheritance rules up to a certain amount). It's the wrapper most used for tax diversification and estate planning, more than for pure performance.

<Callout>
A life insurance contract is not an investment in itself: like the PEA or the CTO, it's a container. Its performance entirely depends on what you put inside it (safe fonds euros vs equity unités de compte).
</Callout>

<Example title="A practical view: what goes where">
An investor who wants broad exposure to global equities for the long term will first use their PEA (via an eligible World ETF), up to the cap or as needed. For direct US stocks the PEA doesn't allow, or to go beyond the PEA cap, they complement it with a CTO. Life insurance comes into play mostly to diversify taxation over the very long term or prepare a wealth transfer, not as the first instinct for investing.
</Example>

<KeyTakeaway label="Key takeaway">
None of these three wrappers is "better" in absolute terms: they serve different needs and complement each other. The PEA for most of your long-term equity exposure with the best tax treatment, the CTO for what the PEA doesn't cover, life insurance for tax diversification and estate planning.
</KeyTakeaway>
