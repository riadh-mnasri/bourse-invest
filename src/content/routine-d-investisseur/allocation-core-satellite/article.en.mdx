Once the basics are understood (stocks, bonds, ETFs, tax wrappers), the next question is structural: how do you actually split your money? The core-satellite structure is a simple, widely used answer that makes individual mistakes harmless to the overall goal.

## The principle

- **The core, 80 to 90% of the portfolio**: one or two broad World ETFs (MSCI World or ACWI type), held with little to no frequent trading. Zero stock-picking here. The goal is to capture the global market's average growth without having to make active decisions.
- **The satellite, 10 to 20% of the portfolio**: the pocket where you can express individual convictions (specific stocks, a sector theme, a more targeted second ETF), deliberately capped.

The satellite's cap isn't arbitrary: it's what guarantees that an individual conviction mistake (a stock that drops, a sector bet that doesn't play out) stays without serious consequence for the overall capitalization goal.

<Callout>
Core-satellite isn't a rigid prescription to follow to the letter: it's an organizing principle. What matters is the reasoning behind it (separating what needs to be reliable from what's allowed to be wrong), not the exact percentages.
</Callout>

## Why separate the two instead of mixing everything

Without this separation, an unlucky individual conviction (a stock that loses 60%) can seriously dent a portfolio's overall performance, especially if it represented a significant share of the total. By capping the satellite's share, the maximum impact of a bad conviction is known in advance and stays manageable.

It also lets you psychologically separate two different activities: serious, regular capitalization (the core, which you almost never look at), and exploration or learning (the satellite, where you can follow an investment thesis closely without putting the whole portfolio at risk).

<Example title="A concrete application with two wrappers">
An investor with both a PEA and a CTO can put the core on the PEA (synthetic World ETF, tax benefit after 5 years, regular automated contributions) and the satellite on the CTO (individually tracked stocks, less favorable tax treatment but access to securities not eligible for the PEA). Each wrapper keeps a clear role instead of overlapping.
</Example>

<KeyTakeaway label="Key takeaway">
A broad, boring core does most of the capitalization work over time. A capped satellite leaves room for exploration without endangering the main goal. The clean separation between the two matters more than the exact percentages chosen.
</KeyTakeaway>
