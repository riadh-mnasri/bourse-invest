Most studies on the actual returns of individual investors show the same gap: the return an average investor actually gets is lower than the return of the fund they hold. The difference is almost never the product choice, it's the behavior around it.

## Panic selling

Selling during a market downturn turns a temporary fluctuation (see the article on market risk) into a permanent loss. It's the most costly bias, because it inverts exactly the logic that makes long-term investing work: buy low, sell high becomes, under panic, sell low.

<Callout tone="warning">
The trigger is almost never a rational calculation about the economic situation. It's the discomfort of seeing a red number on an account statement. A spaced-out review routine (see below) directly limits exposure to that trigger.
</Callout>

## Chasing past performance

Buying what has recently gone up a lot, extrapolating that the trend will continue, is a natural reflex but a statistically unreliable one: an asset's recent past performance doesn't predict its future performance, and the assets that have risen most recently are often the ones whose valuation has become most stretched.

## Over-diversifying out of caution

Piling up ETF or fund lines out of fear of "putting all your eggs in one basket" can look prudent, but often results in illusory diversification: several slightly different World ETFs overlap massively without really reducing risk, while making the portfolio harder to track and rebalance.

## Checking too often

Checking your portfolio daily exposes you to seeing normal volatility framed as a signal worth interpreting, which mechanically increases the temptation to act without good reason. Reviewing more often doesn't make better decisions possible, it just makes more decisions possible, which statistically increases the odds of a mistake.

<Example title="A routine that limits these biases by construction">
Automated monthly contribution (no manual decision, so no room for panic or timing). A 15-minute quarterly review, to check nothing is abnormal, without reacting to normal volatility. Rebalancing and an allocation review once a year. A simple rule before any change outside this rhythm: "if I can't explain this exact risk in one sentence, I don't touch it."
</Example>

<KeyTakeaway label="Key takeaway">
The product chosen (a good, diversified World ETF) matters less, over time, than the discipline around it: not selling in panic, not chasing past performance, not over-diversifying out of anxiety, and deliberately limiting how often you look.
</KeyTakeaway>
